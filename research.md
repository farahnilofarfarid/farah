<section class="section">
  <h2>Dissertation</h2>
  <div class="items">
    <p><em>Passages of Capital: Labour, Credit, and Commerce in the Imperial World Economy, 1890–1914</em><br>
    <span class="meta">Committee: Inés Valdez (chair), Joshua Simon, Glory Liu, Samuel Chambers</span></p>

    <p>My dissertation and book project examines how capitalism and empire shaped each other in the world economy between 1890 and 1914. In political theory, capitalism is usually told as a story made in Europe’s industrial centres and carried outward to the rest of the world. I reconstruct capitalism from the standpoint of circulation rather than production, following the movement of labour, credit, and goods across imperial space. It took shape, I argue, at the peripheries of empire as much as at its centres, through the unequal terms on which those peripheries were incorporated.</p>

    <p>I read empire’s economic records alongside its political texts, extending political theory’s archive to the sources through which the world economy documented its own operation. The first of three chapters studies labour on the grain estates of eastern Prussia, through Max Weber’s 1892 survey of their farm workers. The second studies credit at the India Office in London, which held India’s currency reserves and lent them to banks in the City, through John Maynard Keynes’s <em>Indian Currency and Finance</em> (1913). The third studies trade along the caravan route from Peshawar over the Khyber Pass to Bukhara, through George Curzon’s writings on Central Asia.</p>

    <p>Together, these chapters ask how political theory’s concepts of labour, money, and trade change when imperial relations are taken as their starting point. They also raise a question for the present: how much of what wealthy democracies experience as economic instability reflects conditions long familiar elsewhere? My research contributes to the history of political thought, global political economy, and the histories of empire and capitalism.</p>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="section">
  <h2>Articles</h2>
  <div class="items">
    <div class="entry">
      “Marxism’s Worker, Capitalism’s Labour: Labour Politics and Labour Deployment in Imperial Germany, 1890–1914”<br>
      <span class="meta">Under review</span>
      <details>
        <summary>Abstract</summary>
        <p>Recent political theory has revived the wage worker as the figure through which to understand and contest capitalism, while setting aside the twentieth-century debates in which that figure was tested against how labour was actually organized. This article returns to that test through a puzzle in the East Elbian estates that Max Weber surveyed in 1892, in a study that remains untranslated. As landowners produced for competitive markets, they combined tenancies and payments in kind with the seasonal recruitment of foreign workers. The German Social Democrats expected capitalist agriculture to produce a class of wage workers with increasingly common interests. What Weber observed instead was that differences in residence rights and household obligations gave employers reasons to keep several forms of labour on the same estate. Drawing on Jairus Banaji, the article argues that political theory should understand capitalism through labour deployment, the varied arrangements, shaped by employers, markets and states, through which labour is recruited, controlled and paid, rather than define it by free wage labour.</p>
      </details>
    </div>

    <div class="entry">
      “Cheap Money”<br>
      <span class="meta">In preparation</span>
      <details>
        <summary>Abstract</summary>
        <p>Political theory has recently recovered money and credit as political institutions, but it treats them within a single polity, while theories of empire have centred sovereignty, law and labour and left credit aside. This article brings the two together by examining the imperial foundations of British financial power through John Maynard Keynes’s writings from his years at the India Office, especially <em>Indian Currency and Finance</em> (1913). It reconstructs how Council Bills, Home Charges and the London-held Gold Standard Reserve connected Indian revenue to metropolitan credit and underwrote the City of London’s liquidity and its central role in financing world trade and investment. It argues that monetary management, the public control over currency, credit and reserves associated with democratic self-government, is not simply a domestic capacity but a relational one, embedded in a hierarchical global distribution of monetary power.</p>
      </details>
    </div>

    <div class="entry">
      “Jealousy of Trade at the Frontier”<br>
      <span class="meta">In preparation</span>
      <details>
        <summary>Abstract</summary>
        <p>Political theory has largely set commerce aside in its account of capitalism and empire, inheriting a liberal view of trade as a force for peace among nations. This article restores commerce through the Anglo-Russian rivalry on the North-West Frontier between 1890 and 1914, following the caravan route from Peshawar over the Khyber Pass to Bukhara across three lines of imperial authority, at each of which passage was priced and controlled. Drawing on consular reports, tariffs and railway statistics, George Curzon traced how Britain and Russia competed to direct a trade neither controlled in full. His account bears out István Hont’s understanding of commercial rivalry as a lasting condition of modern states. Yet those who carried the trade belonged to neither empire, and their prosperity scarcely figured in either power’s reckoning of what it stood to gain. The article argues that the frontier reveals a limit common to Hont’s account and to the liberal claim that trade could help poorer nations catch up with richer ones: both assess trade in terms of national fortunes, leaving open what it meant for the people through whose territories it passed and on whose labour it depended.</p>
      </details>
    </div>

<section class="section">
  <h2>Second Project</h2>
  <div class="items">
    <p>“Theorizing Global Capitalism from the Postcolonial South: Mahbub ul Haq, the World Bank, and the New International Economic Order”<br>
    <span class="meta">Supported by an APSA Centennial Center Summer Research Grant, 2026</span></p>

    <p>My second project examines postcolonial efforts to change the terms of participation in the world economy. Its first stage centers on Mahbub ul Haq, the Pakistani World Bank economist later known as the architect of the Human Development Index, and his part in debates over a New International Economic Order, including his proposal for an international central bank that would issue a world currency and allocate credit according to development needs rather than existing wealth. Haq concluded late in life that developing countries had “reached out for international justice while denying economic justice to their own people.” Reading his writings alongside the World Bank Group Archives and UNCTAD’s records, I examine how he connected international monetary reform to domestic redistribution, and where these ambitions came into tension.</p>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="section">
  <h2>Third Project</h2>
  <div class="items">
    <p>Nature and Freedom</p>

    <p>A third strand of my research concerns the relationship between nature and freedom in Aristotle, Hegel and Marx, and the place of philosophy of nature in their social and political thought. I am interested in how they understand human beings as both natural and historical beings, and what this means for the organization of collective life. The project’s first paper, “Political Organicism: An Intellectual Lineage through Aristotle, Hegel, and Marx,” approaches these questions through Marx’s critique of Hegel, asking how the separation of state and civil society unsettles the conception of political community as an organic whole. I am currently preparing it for publication.</p>
  </div>
